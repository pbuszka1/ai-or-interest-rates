# Limitations: the full list

The paper gives a short version in "What this can't tell us." This is the complete list, grouped by what each limitation threatens. Sources are cited by author and year and listed at the end.

The claim this study can support is deliberately narrow. I group the weaknesses below by what they threaten, and state what each would take to resolve.

## Threats to Identification

- **Relative identification.** Every design compares more exposed with less exposed work, and the low-exposure industry controls still average about 0.27 on the GPT-4 rating rather than zero. If AI affects all industries, or shifts work between them, these designs understate the effect. They can show that exposed work did not do worse, but not that the economy as a whole was unaffected. Resolving this would require a macroeconomic model or cross-country comparisons.
- **Contaminated comparison groups.** Within a sector, AI could shift work from exposed occupations to less exposed ones. That would widen the gap between them even if total employment did not change, and it would mean the comparison group is itself affected by the treatment.
- **Occupation-specific shocks.** Test B's fixed effects do not absorb shocks that differ by occupation within a sector, including differences in rate sensitivity (Zens, Böck and Zörner 2020), Section 174's focus on R&D staff, and an uneven pandemic boom. The age design has a related problem: any slowdown in an occupation's hiring lowers young workers' employment relative to older workers, so it cannot by itself separate AI from other causes of that slowdown.
- **Education and skill.** Exposed occupations tend to be more educated, and education is the one control that attenuates the results in (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen 2026). Adding college share by year, an exploratory check, leaves Test B near zero (−0.5%) but widens its interval to −8.4% to +7.4%, and it moves the young-worker estimate to −5.5% (p = 0.31). A control this coarse may not capture the full difference. A long-running shift toward educated work could mask a decline, while a downturn among educated workers could mimic one.
- **Observational equivalence.** "The boom unwound" and "AI prevented the boom from lasting" predict the same employment path, and the boom control in Test A cannot distinguish them. The evidence against the second story is indirect. A mechanism test on productivity is the most direct way to separate them.
- **Sensitivity to the pre-trend.** Test A fails its pre-trend test (p = 0.073), and its answer moves from −5.0% to −6.1% under a linear trend extrapolation. With a continuous exposure dose, comparing effects across exposure levels also needs a stronger parallel-trends assumption than a simple treated-versus-untreated comparison (Callaway, Goodman-Bacon and Sant'Anna, NBER w32117). That is the simplest case of (Rambachan and Roth 2023); a full sensitivity analysis under that framework is not part of this study. A reader who weights that exploratory estimate more heavily than I do could reasonably see more evidence of an AI effect at the industry level than I report. Passing a pre-trend test, as Test B does, also does not prove parallel trends, because such tests have limited power.
- **Concurrent shocks.** Beyond rates and the boom, the post-period overlaps with Section 174 and its reversal, changes in immigration policy, return-to-office mandates and the 2025 tariffs. Any of these could affect exposed work differently within a sector.
- **An imperfect placebo.** The dot-com bust was a valuation bubble during which the Federal Reserve cut rates, whereas 2022 involved rising rates, and the dot-com model fails its own pre-trend test. A similar pattern suggests that exposed industries can fall this way without AI. It does not prove that 2022 had the same cause.

## Limitations of the AI Exposure Measure

This may be the most important limitation in the paper, because every exposure comparison depends on a single measure. This study observes no data on how AI is actually used. It relies on the scores in (Eloundou et al. 2024), which estimate which tasks a language model could speed up. The results are therefore closer to an intent-to-treat estimate: they capture the effect of being in a job AI could affect, not the effect of AI being used in that job. If adoption among exposed workers is low or uneven, a real effect among actual users could be diluted beyond detection.

- **Potential, not adoption.** The authors state that they make no predictions about the "development or adoption timeline" of these models and that the paper does not systematically analyze adoption (Eloundou et al. 2024). They also identify tasks where adoption may lag capability, such as those requiring several people to change their habits, or those where regulation or norms call for human oversight.
- **A static snapshot.** The ratings reflect an early version of GPT-4 and capabilities as understood in 2023, yet I apply them unchanged through 2026. The authors describe their projections as forward-looking and subject to change (Eloundou et al. 2024). The jump in capability since then, especially in agents, may mean the scores misrank today's exposure. Industry scores also hold the occupation mix fixed at 2015–19.
- **Subjective labels.** The authors call the subjectivity of labeling "a fundamental limitation" (Eloundou et al. 2024). Their annotators knew LLMs well but were not occupationally diverse, and were mostly unaware of which occupations the activities they rated belonged to.
- **Rater disagreement.** Neither source is ground truth (Eloundou et al. 2024). At the task level, GPT-4 and human beta ratings agree only 65.6% of the time (Pearson correlation 0.591). Agreement is much higher once tasks are aggregated: by my calculation on the scores used here, the two beta ratings correlate at 0.89 across occupations and 0.91 across the occupation groups in Test B. The choice of measure can matter a great deal: under the strict alpha measure, software developers score 0.79 from GPT-4 and 0.05 from human raters. I use beta and average the two ratings to reduce this problem, but that does not remove it.
- **Instability across models.** Yin, Vu and Persico (Yin, Vu and Persico 2026) find that three frontier models given the same rubric produce mean exposure scores differing by a factor of 3.6, with agreement as low as 57%. Difference-in-differences coefficients varied 2.4-fold, and county-level results changed direction depending on the annotator. They characterize this as non-classical measurement error. Classical noise would bias my estimates toward zero, but non-classical error has no guaranteed direction, so I cannot claim the true effect must be larger than what I report.
- **The task framework itself.** Scores assume that an occupation can be broken down into separable tasks. The authors note that this may omit tacit skills and dependencies between tasks, and that if the framework is not a valid representation of the work, the exposure analysis "would largely be invalidated" (Eloundou et al. 2024).
- **Arbitrary thresholds.** A task counts as exposed if an LLM could reduce its completion time by at least 50% at equivalent quality. The authors acknowledge that this threshold is somewhat arbitrary and that real-world time savings are likely lower (Eloundou et al. 2024).
- **Only one measure was run.** The pre-registration called for rerunning with alternatives, including each rating alone and the AIOE measure of Felten et al. (Felten, Raj and Seamans 2021) Those reruns are not part of this study, so I cannot show that these results hold under other measures. This matters because exposure measures can capture quite different things.
- **Usage data would have its own gaps.** Replacing exposure with usage would not fully solve this. The Anthropic Economic Index covers only conversations with one company's model, and its users are not a random sample of workers, skewing toward software. Other tools, enterprise deployments and uses outside that platform are not observed. The Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey is reported at the firm and sector level rather than by occupation, and its question wording changed in November 2025. Usage is also measured after 2023, so employment changes could drive usage rather than the reverse.

## Other Measurement Threats

- **Not technology hiring.** The main outcome is the stock of employment across all occupations and private industries. Employment moves more slowly than hiring, so a large fall in hiring in one sector could leave these estimates little changed. These results cannot confirm or reject any specific account of technology hiring.
- **Level of aggregation.** Test A cannot detect reallocation inside an industry. Test B can, but its 14 sectors are broad and may still mask within-firm changes of the kind studied in (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen 2026).
- **Limited outcomes.** AI could first appear in hours, wages, new-hire pay or job quality, none of which are reported here.
- **Survey noise.** Test B depends on CPS cells with small samples, so the same survey underlies both its null and the 2026 decline among young workers.
- **Sample selection.** Test B keeps only cells with enough respondents in every year, including post-ChatGPT years, so cells that shrank can drop out. Applying the filter only through 2019 changes both the all-age and young-worker results little (Sections 6.2 and 6.3).
- **Occupation coding.** The CPS moved to 2018 occupation codes in January 2020. BLS later corrected its January–July 2020 estimates after programming errors in an edit that checked occupations against reported education (BLS 2020 correction notice), and OCC2010 maps the newer codes back to 2010 categories through a harmonization that its documentation describes as imperfect in places (IPUMS OCC2010 notes). Test B's main contrast falls after the switch and its 2012–18 pre-trend test falls before it, but the step from the 2019 reference year to later years crosses it, as does the every-year cell filter.

## Threats to Statistical Conclusions

- **Power for age effects.** The Round 1 age designs detect a 15% step only about 40% of the time, and the triple difference received no injection test, while (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen 2026) reports a 19% shortfall on its preferred measure. A failure to find a clear post-ChatGPT widening among young workers is therefore weak evidence.
- **Short post-period.** Adoption is gradual and recent, particularly for agents, and the data ends in September 2026. An effect that is still building may not yet be detectable.
- **Weak single-unit fit.** The computer systems design estimate relies heavily on one donor (home health care, 31% of the weight), and the industry is far more cyclical than its synthetic control. That result should not be read as more than suggestive in either direction.
- **Pre-pandemic drift in hiring.** The QWI age gap trends downward for years before 2020. I read the gap as preceding ChatGPT, but this drift also means the 2019 baseline is not a stable reference point, so the exact size of the estimate should be treated with caution.
- **Data at the edge.** The 2026 figures cover January through September, CES is revised each February, and the CPS skipped October 2025. The CPS counts used here are not seasonally adjusted, so comparing part of 2026 with full years can distort groups with seasonal patterns, such as recent graduates. Using January–September in every year leaves Test B at +0.2% but reduces the young-worker estimate to −2.7%. The newest figures, where an AI effect would appear first, are the least settled.

## Researcher Degrees of Freedom

- **Choices made after seeing data.** The 2019 baseline, the seasonal adjustment of QWI and the exclusion of pandemic quarters were all decided after seeing some results. Each is logged with its rationale, and the alternative is reported. A specification curve would measure how much these choices matter, but it is not part of this study.
- **An imperfect pre-registration.** Round 2 reuses CES and CPS data I had already examined in other designs, so its specifications may have been unconsciously shaped by Round 1. Test A follows a plan written before Round 1 ran, but the age version revisits a decline I had already seen.
- **Departures from the pre-registration.** Secondary tests on actual AI use, productivity, Section 174 and earnings, the specification curves, and the ACS rerun were not run, and no results are withheld. Because of this, the coherence and robustness conditions in the verdict rules were never reached. The first run of Test A also used a 2020–25 rather than 2015–19 occupation mix, which has since been corrected (Section 5).
